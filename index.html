Designing front aero for my 2015 WRX STI

Zakary Miles

I was getting my 2015 Subaru WRX STI ready for the track, so I decided to design and build my own front aero. I modeled the look I wanted in SolidWorks, with a wide splitter and two sharp, Varis-inspired canards at each corner. Then I used Flow Simulation to see how much front downforce I could gain with that shape and what it would cost in drag.

Contents
Designing around the bumper Flow testing at 70 mph Centerline pressure Cutting and fitting the ABS On the car Dimensioned drawings

Designing around the bumper

I started with a 75 in (1,905 mm) tape measurement under the car to set the nominal splitter width. The existing lip drops 1.5 in (38.1 mm) below the bumper. In CAD I placed the splitter 105 mm above the road and its leading edge 61 mm ahead of the lip at the centerline. Those two estimates set the gap under the splitter and how much of it projects into the airflow.

I traced the upper canard from a high-angle photo of my bumper. It has a short, straight front edge, a clipped corner below the fog light, and an upturned outer edge toward the wheel. The lower blade follows the same outline with a slightly larger footprint. I estimated a nose-down pitch of 16° for the upper blade and 15° for the lower one from the available photos.

Flow testing at 70 mph

I ran the same simplified STI model with steady airflow straight ahead at 70 mph (31.3 m/s). I started with the lip alone, added the splitter and endplates, then added the stacked canards. Each result is the mean of the final 20 recorded iterations.

Test conditions

I used zero yaw, 101,325 Pa ambient pressure, and a temperature of 293.15 K. The model had a simplified underbody and four stationary tires. The computational domain extended from −7 to 7 m laterally, from −0.05 to 8 m vertically, and from −27 to 12 m along the forward axis.

All three runs used the same Boolean mesh setting and nominal mesh level 2, producing 174,207 to 174,262 fluid cells. I integrated pressure and shear over the vehicle and aero surfaces, excluding the stationary road. I used the pitching moment about the point on the road below the front axle and the 2,650 mm wheelbase to calculate equivalent front and rear axle loads.

Cutting and fitting the ABS

I transferred the CAD dimensions to ABS sheet, cut the parts with a rotary cutter, and sanded the edges. I held each piece against my bumper to check the fit, then trimmed and sanded the places that needed a little more clearance. Each canard sits on an L-bracket with one leg bolted to the bumper and the other supporting the blade from below. Inboard angle brackets join the endplates to the splitter.

Design and verification of a three-axis desktop robot

I designed a three-axis robot to reach 425 mm from a fixed desk mount while keeping its sensor head clear of the surface. A collision at the original lower tilt limit drove a joint-limit revision. Reading the margin

The nominal case clears the 1.5 design target by 0.33. At the assumed 0.981 N·m rating, a 1.5 margin allows about 0.654 N·m of motor demand. That leaves approximately 0.119 N·m above the nominal 0.535 N·m estimate. Weighing the moving assembly and locating its center of gravity will narrow that uncertainty before a motor is qualified for continuous operation.

Campus trolley bike rack · Solo design study · Static analysis complete

Structural design and mounting loads of a campus trolley bicycle rack

Zakary Miles

I modeled a welded carrier for two bicycles and compared a cantilever calculation with a static finite element model. Both indicate low stress under the 110 lbf load. The SOLIDWORKS mass estimate of 126.401 kg puts the empty rack at approximately 2.53 times its payload. That result makes the allowable vehicle moment and a gravity-inclusive fixture test central to the next iteration.

Contents
Design envelope Assembly plan Mounting loads Static simulation Interpreting the nodal stress traces Weight and verification Conclusion and source records

Design envelope

The brief called for carrying two adult bicycles across the back of a campus trolley. I set the combined bicycle load at 110 lbf. Two channels locate the tires, and a curved rail gives the rider a place to strap each frame.

Interfaces that set the envelope

The channel positions define the fore-aft load locations. The curved rail provides a tie point, so the retention load follows a different path from the vertical wheel load. Before release, the vehicle bolt pattern, rear access and clearances would need to be measured on the actual trolley.

Assembly plan

I chose annealed 201 stainless steel and planned a welded assembly with a bolted vehicle interface. Wheel loads pass from the two tire channels through four support beams into one mounting frame. The curved rail provides the strap attachment.

Proposed fabrication sequence

  1. Saw the support stock to length and machine the mounting holes and two tire channels.
  2. Form the curved rail, position the channels on the beams, and MIG weld the assembly.
  3. Check bolt access and alignment at the trolley before final installation.

The original $58.56 material estimate used a scrap price of $0.21/lb. Stock, machining time, weld labor and mounting hardware would need separate quotes for a build.

Part Qty Role in the planned assembly
Tire channel 2 Receives the wheel loads and locates the tires.
Support beam 4 Transfers channel loads into the frame.
Mounting frame 1 Bolted interface to the trolley.
Curved rail 1 Tie point for bicycle frames.

The original process plan used a bandsaw for stock preparation, a CNC mill for the holes and channel pockets, a forming die for the rail, and MIG welding for the fixed joints. Machining a channel from block stock removes material already paid for. A formed section is a candidate for the next mass and cost comparison.

Mounting loads

I simplified the support to a cantilever with two 55 lbf loads. Their centers lie 5.25 in and 23.75 in behind the fixed end. Together they produce a bending moment of 1,595 lbf·in at the mount.

Calculation at the fixed end

With the two load centers at 5.25 in and 23.75 in, the root reaction is 110 lbf and the moment is 55 × 5.25 + 55 × 23.75 = 1,595 lbf·in. The idealized 2 in square section has I = bh³/12 ≈ 1.333 in⁴ and c = 1 in. The worksheet rounds I to 1.33 in⁴. Using σ = Mc/I gives approximately 1,199 psi. Dividing 38,000 psi by that stress gives the reported 31.68.

This local beam calculation omits bolt preload, contact compliance and weld geometry. The forces at the actual fasteners also depend on their spacing and the stiffness of the trolley bracket.

Static simulation

I distributed the same 110 lbf over four wheel-load locations in the assembly model and constrained the mounting region. The stress plot peaks at about 966 psi. With a modeled yield strength of 42,350 psi, the static factor of safety is about 44.

Reconciling the two estimates

The hand check uses a single beam and a lower strength value. Using 42,350 psi in both calculations gives about 35.3 for the beam and 43.8 for the assembly. The remaining difference is worth investigating at the same section with matching restraints. Neither calculation measures bolt slip, weld quality or bicycle retention.

Boundary conditions and uncertainty

The 966 psi peak is specific to the applied wheel forces and idealized mounting restraints. The source includes a dynamic stress plot but omits its excitation history. It also gives no mesh-convergence series. Neither the static plot nor the dynamic image establishes fatigue life or strap retention during travel.

Interpreting the nodal stress traces

The source study also plots von Mises stress at a series of mesh locations. The static trace rises to approximately 220 psi near the beginning of the sampled locations and falls toward 20 psi farther along. The assembly contour reaches 966 psi. These numbers describe different selections of the model. A trace through selected nodes can miss the global maximum.

The dynamic trace is a spatial sample at one instant. Its local peak is about 165 psi. To evaluate motion on the trolley, I would first define a vehicle acceleration history and identify the governing instant and location over the whole run. These two curves alone cannot establish a dynamic amplification factor, a fatigue margin or secure bicycle retention.

Weight and verification

SOLIDWORKS calculated a mass of 126.401 kg, about 279 lb empty. With both bicycles, the mount would carry about 389 lbf at rest. The original study documents the bicycle load but leaves the gravity setting unspecified. The reported margin cannot establish that the installation can support that combined load.

Proposed checks before vehicle use

I completed the SOLIDWORKS model and load analysis. Physical load, retention and road tests remain open.

  1. Establish the mount limits. Obtain the trolley’s allowable rear load and moment, bolt pattern and clearance envelope. Use those limits to set a rack mass budget.
  2. Compare lighter sections. Model formed channels in place of the machined stock. Include gravity and check the empty, one-bike and two-bike cases. Record mass, center of gravity, mounting reactions and tip deflection for each.
  3. Check the prediction on a fixture. Weigh the prototype. Apply known loads at the wheel positions and measure channel deflection, joint slip and residual set after unloading. Compare with the model and agreed mount limits before planning vibration and strap-retention tests.

How the mount load scales

For the worksheet’s bicycle positions, a gravity-inclusive static moment would be 1,595 + 279d lbf·in, where d is the rearward distance in inches from the mounting plane to the rack center of gravity. Each additional inch of offset adds about 279 lbf·in. The original envelope gives the overall depth but does not locate that center, so a single installed moment cannot be calculated from the slides.

Conclusion and source records

The study answers a narrow but useful question. The idealized supports carry the specified bicycle load with stresses well below yield. The 279 lb empty rack changes the integration decision. I would establish the mount’s allowable force and moment, revise the section geometry against that budget, and use a fixture to check deflection and slip before any road trial.

From a chart to a job

I built the ingestion pipeline, FastAPI service, PostgreSQL data model, and React interface. A scheduled workflow imports postings from Greenhouse and Lever, runs extraction and deduplication, and records market snapshots. The frontend and API were deployed through Vercel and Render.

Extracting the evidence

I kept each raw posting so a correction to the extractor could be applied to older data. The first version used an Aho–Corasick matcher to search roughly 600 aliases in one pass. Short names such as “R” needed checks for case, boundaries, and nearby technical terms. Section headings helped distinguish a required skill from one in a wish list. An accepted match retained the sentence that triggered it.

On 19 July 2026, the first full run processed 12,217 postings. It found at least one technology in 8,524, extracted compensation in 8,595, and mapped 3,948 titles to standard job families. Those counts describe separate fields and overlap.

One error surfaced in the aggregate. A chart put C at roughly 20% because the matcher had picked up the final letter of “U.S.C.” in repeated legal boilerplate. After checking the stored sentences, I tightened the single-letter rule, incremented the extractor version, and reprocessed the corpus.

Counting the market

The search results and analytics use the same filter definition for role and geography. A posting with several matching locations contributes once. Cross-source duplicates are grouped conservatively, so two openings with similar titles do not silently become one.

In the market test fixture, Python appears in 13 of 23 postings. Three postings have no detected technology and still belong in the denominator. That puts Python’s share at 56.5%. Dropping those three would inflate it to 65%. Eleven postings actually require Python.

Location and radius queries have too many combinations to precompute. I cached exact queries between data refreshes and saved daily snapshots for a bounded set of markets. The analytics design record measured about 100–310 ms for a cold national query over roughly 12,000 jobs and about 13 ms for a cached response.

Explaining a match

My scoring model weights skills at 55%, title at 25%, and experience at 20%. A required skill carries twice the weight of a preferred skill. When a component cannot be evaluated, its weight is redistributed across the available components.

One regression case asks for three required skills and two preferred skills. The candidate has two required skills and one preferred skill, yielding 5 of 8 weighted points. With a matching title and sufficient experience, the score rounds to 79 out of 100. The explanation names Kafka and Terraform as the gaps so the candidate can decide whether to apply.

Validation and limits

A local evaluation on 22 November 2026 compared the extractor’s output with a frozen set of 31 hand-labeled documents.

The small labeled set was developed alongside the extractor. The legal-citation error illustrates what that evaluation can miss. Broader accuracy estimates would need an independently labeled sample across employers, including jobs with missing or ambiguous requirements. The market figures also reflect the boards covered by the pipeline.

MediaFlow: building a reliable file conversion workspace

Zakary Miles

Converting one clip should take a few clicks. Converting a folder of clips raises different questions. Can I close the browser while the files process? Can I keep the originals if a conversion fails? I built the workspace, API, storage layer, and worker around a persistent record of each file and job.

Workspace ownership

Quick Convert gives a visitor one file, one conversion, and one download. A temporary workspace adds folders, batch conversion, and ZIP export for the current browser session. A signed-in user can keep a workspace across sessions. All three use the same conversion engine.

Saving a temporary workspace changes its owner and type in place. Files, jobs, and stored objects retain their identifiers. A user who decides to keep a batch can pick up where they left off without uploading the sources again.

From input to output

The browser sends an upload to FastAPI. The API identifies the file from its contents and validates the requested output against a shared format registry. It records a pending job in PostgreSQL and returns HTTP 202. The client can then query the job for progress and, once complete, its download URL.

I used the same registry for the browser’s target menu and the worker’s converter selection. Counting the August registry gives 48 input formats and 437 source-to-target pairs. For example, a video can be converted to another container or have its audio extracted. The registry routes video and audio to FFmpeg, images to ImageMagick, and office documents to the appropriate document engine.

A worker reads the source from local storage or stages a remote object on disk, runs the selected converter, and stores the result separately from the original. When FFmpeg reports a usable duration, the worker can turn elapsed media time into a percentage. Otherwise the interface displays progress without a percentage.

Claiming and recovering jobs

I used PostgreSQL for the queue so the workspace can keep the file record and conversion state together. Each worker searches for one eligible job. On PostgreSQL the query locks the row and skips work already locked by another worker. The claim commits before conversion starts, leaving the database transaction short even for a long video.

Moving large files

The local filesystem is the default storage backend. With S3-compatible storage, uploads of 64 MiB or more go directly from the browser to the object store by default. The API coordinates three steps.

  1. Reserve space. The API checks the workspace quota, counts outstanding reservations, and returns a short-lived upload URL.
  2. Transfer the bytes. The browser uploads directly to the assigned object key. The workspace still has no file record for this upload.
  3. Verify and commit. The API checks ownership, actual object size, remaining quota, and media type. A successful check creates the file record. A size mismatch triggers cleanup of the object and its reservation.

Counting outstanding reservations matters when two uploads start at once. Each might fit individually while their combined size exceeds the quota. Expired reservations are reclaimed so an abandoned browser tab cannot hold space indefinitely.

Validation and limits

A local run on 22 November 2026 covered three backend test modules for the job queue, worker scratch files, and direct-upload API. 32 tests passed and two were skipped. The run used SQLite and a test storage backend. It checked behavior during interrupted work, cancellation, mismatched upload sizes, and concurrent attempts to finalize the same upload.

The two skipped cases require FFmpeg and FFprobe. They exercise rejection of a real corrupt media file and an end-to-end conversion after direct upload. The 437 registry entries describe supported routes. This run did not execute every conversion pair.

The S3-compatible implementation was exercised locally with MinIO during development. Production worker throughput, cloud transfer latency, and recovery time under concurrent load remain unmeasured. A useful next test would stop one of two PostgreSQL-backed workers mid-conversion and record the time until the other produces a single downloadable output.
